STATUS REPORT
Quantum Circuit Optimization with Genetic Algorithms (PFE)
Date: 2026-08-26 (added: both root causes of the scaling stall now
found and fixed, both with verified zero behavior change -- see
CURRENT STATUS)

=====================================================================
OBJECTIVE
=====================================================================
Make quantum circuit optimization scale to larger circuits than
standard methods allow. Approach: split a target circuit into
qubit blocks, use a multi-objective genetic algorithm to evolve a
cheaper/shallower circuit per block that still reproduces the
original's behavior with high fidelity, then reassemble. Matters
for near-term (noisy, limited-connectivity) quantum devices, where
shallower accurate circuits are hard to get via standard,
non-partitioned optimization.

Given the ~4-month, laptop-only timeline to a paper (submission
~Dec 2026), the current phase validates the approach and research
contribution at a fixed, tractable scale (8 qubits) before chasing
larger circuits. Scaling up is a planned follow-on, not abandoned.

=====================================================================
CURRENT STATUS
=====================================================================
Pipeline (partition -> per-block GA optimize -> reassemble ->
re-inject lost cross-block gates -> compress) is implemented and
working, with engineering infrastructure to run it reliably at
scale on a laptop: a CLI experiment runner, a resumable multi-seed
sweep runner (crash-isolated subprocesses), a results aggregator,
and a pytest smoke-test suite.

Benchmark suite: 5 standard circuit families (random weakly-
connected, QAOA MaxCut, W-state, QFT, hardware-efficient ansatz),
fixed at 8 qubits. Pushing to 12-20 qubits repeatedly hit
performance walls (hangs / 1hr+ runs) on genuinely entangled
circuits (QAOA-style) -- paused and deferred rather than debugged
further at the time; see NEXT STEPS.

Update: profiled where those stalls actually came from (rather than
continuing to guess) and found one clear, fixable cause -- one of the
two re-injection strategies (simulated annealing) was repeatedly
re-checking its own starting circuit's accuracy from scratch,
thousands of times per run, even though that check's answer never
actually depended on the starting circuit at all. Replaced it with a
direct calculation that skips the redundant work entirely. Verified
this gives the exact same result as before (not an approximation --
same energies, same decisions, same output), and turns a run that
previously didn't finish in 10 minutes into one that finishes in
under a second at the same settings; at the settings the pipeline
actually uses by default, a 14- and 20-qubit run that used to hang
for hours each now complete in about 7 seconds.

Update 2: did the same investigation for the other re-injection
strategy (the greedy one, which currently dominates the final result
per the finding below). Its problem was different -- it was rebuilding
and re-checking the ENTIRE candidate circuit's accuracy from scratch
on every one of up to 300 attempts per run, when only a cached,
reusable version of that check plus a small local update was actually
needed each time. Fixed the same way: verified the new version gives
the exact same accepted/rejected list as before, then measured the
speedup. A 12-qubit run that previously did not finish in 5 minutes
now completes in 26 seconds; a 13-qubit run, previously also stuck,
completes in about 2.5 minutes. Both re-injection strategies are now
fixed. What's left before larger circuits are practical again is a
separate, deeper problem: past roughly 13-14 qubits, even a single
accuracy check itself (independent of how many times it's called)
becomes too slow by other means already known about (see
IMPLEMENTATION NOTES) -- these two fixes remove the "calling it too
many times" problem but not that one.

Research contribution chosen and implemented: a second per-block
algorithm, SMS-EMOA (optimizes for a diverse set of good trade-off
circuits, vs. NSGA-II's single-best focus), plus two ablations:
mutation-operator combinations, and an optional local fine-tuning
pass after the GA search. 260 total runs across all comparisons,
in runs/results_master.csv. Findings:
- NSGA-II vs SMS-EMOA: NSGA-II finds the single best circuit
  slightly more often; SMS-EMOA finds a wider, more diverse set of
  good trade-offs at no extra compute time. Neither wins outright --
  reported as complementary.
- Mutation operators: adding a "delete" option alongside "swap" and
  "add" gives a better accuracy/size balance than "swap+add" alone
  (matches a recent published result). But the project's original,
  simple mutation operator still performs best overall under NSGA-II,
  so it stays the default there.
- Local fine-tuning pass: gives a small, real accuracy gain at the
  per-block level, but that gain gets overshadowed by the later
  re-injection step, which dominates the final accuracy number at
  this scale -- a useful negative finding, not a failed feature.
- Re-running the mutation/fine-tuning ablations against SMS-EMOA
  showed the best settings are algorithm-specific, not universal:
  SMS-EMOA prefers "swap+add+delete" (not the simple default), and
  its fine-tuning gain is small but more consistently positive than
  under NSGA-II. The paper will need to report each algorithm's own
  best-paired settings rather than one universal configuration.
- Cross-cutting takeaway: at 8 qubits, per-block optimization
  choices matter less to the final result than the re-injection
  step. Will inform whether results are reported at the per-block
  level (where real differences show) vs. only end-to-end.

=====================================================================
IMPLEMENTATION NOTES
=====================================================================
Stack: Qiskit (circuit construction/simulation) + DEAP (NSGA-II /
NSGA-III / hand-rolled SMS-EMOA multi-objective search). Reference
pipeline: M1_finale/final_m1_script.py.

Fidelity (the accuracy metric optimized against) is computed exactly
at 8 qubits -- cheap and fast, not currently an engineering concern.
An approximate SWAP-test/tensor-network estimator for larger circuits
was built and works for weakly-connected circuits up to 20 qubits,
but gets expensive again for highly-entangled ones (cost scales with
entanglement, not just qubit count) -- known, deferred problem for
when scaling resumes.
