STATUS REPORT
Quantum Circuit Optimization with Genetic Algorithms (PFE)
Date: 2026-08-25

=====================================================================
OBJECTIVE
=====================================================================
The goal of this project is to make quantum circuit optimization
scale to larger circuits than standard methods currently allow.

Given a target quantum circuit, we split it into smaller blocks of
qubits, then use a genetic algorithm (multi-objective evolutionary
search) to evolve, for each block, a cheaper and shallower circuit
that still reproduces the original circuit's behavior with high
fidelity. The blocks are then reassembled into a full circuit that
is less costly to run while remaining close to the original.

This targets a practical need in near-term quantum computing:
today's devices are noisy and have limited qubit connectivity, so
shallower, cheaper circuits that preserve accuracy are directly
valuable, and are hard to obtain with standard (non-partitioned,
single-objective) optimization approaches at scale.

Given the ~4-month, laptop-only timeline to a paper submission, the
current phase deliberately validates the approach and research
contribution at a fixed, comfortably tractable scale (8 qubits) first,
rather than continuing to chase larger circuit sizes before that
contribution is decided. Pushing to larger circuits is a real, planned
follow-on goal, not an abandoned one -- see "NEXT STEPS" below.

=====================================================================
CURRENT STATUS
=====================================================================
The core optimization pipeline is implemented and working: circuit
partitioning, per-block evolutionary optimization, reassembly, and
a re-injection step that restores accuracy lost during partitioning.

Over the past weeks, effort has focused on turning this from a
research pipeline into something that can reliably produce results
for a paper on a laptop-only compute budget: reproducible
experiment runs, automated multi-run sweeps across circuit types and
problem sizes, and result aggregation for analysis.

A benchmark suite covering several standard quantum circuit types
(used in quantum chemistry, optimization, and communication
applications) has been built and run at a fixed 8-qubit scale: 50
completed runs (5 circuit families x 2 injection strategies x 5
seeds), producing a real, complete dataset. Attempts to push the same
suite to larger circuit sizes (12/16/20 qubits) repeatedly hit
performance walls in the fidelity/accuracy computation for circuits
with genuine, non-trivial entanglement (e.g. QAOA-style circuits) --
some configurations took over an hour or stalled outright. Rather than
keep spending time debugging that, the decision was made to pause it
and move the research forward on the complete 8-qubit dataset now in
hand; scaling to larger sizes is deferred to a later, dedicated effort
(see NEXT STEPS).

This week, the specific research contribution for the paper was
decided and implemented: an alternative per-block optimization
algorithm (SMS-EMOA, which explicitly optimizes for a well-distributed
set of good trade-off circuits rather than a single best one) was
added alongside the existing NSGA-II approach, plus two supporting
comparison studies -- different combinations of ways an individual
circuit design can be randomly altered during the search ("mutation
operators"), and whether adding a short local fine-tuning pass after
the genetic search helps. All three comparisons were run on the
existing 8-qubit benchmark suite (125 additional runs). Results:
- The new algorithm (SMS-EMOA) tends to find a wider range of good
  trade-off circuits than the original approach (NSGA-II), at no extra
  computing time -- but NSGA-II still tends to find the single best
  circuit slightly more often. Neither is a clear winner; both will be
  reported as complementary options.
- Comparing mutation operator combinations confirmed a result from a
  recent published study: adding a "delete part of the design" option
  alongside "swap" and "add" gives a better balance of accuracy vs.
  circuit size than "swap and add" alone. However, the simple approach
  already used throughout this project's pipeline still performs best
  overall on this specific problem, so it remains the default.
- The local fine-tuning pass does what it's supposed to at the
  individual-block level (small, real accuracy gains there), but those
  gains get overshadowed by the pipeline's later re-injection step,
  which currently dominates the final result at this accuracy level --
  a genuine, useful negative finding rather than a failed feature.
- A cross-cutting takeaway from all three comparisons: at the current
  8-qubit scale, the choices made during per-block optimization matter
  less to the final result than the choices made in the later
  re-injection step. This will inform how results are presented in the
  paper (likely reporting some comparisons at the per-block level,
  where the real differences are visible, rather than only on the
  final end-to-end accuracy number).

=====================================================================
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
=====================================================================
Stack: Qiskit for circuit construction/simulation, DEAP for the
evolutionary algorithm (NSGA-II / NSGA-III multi-objective search).

Pipeline stages (M1_finale/final_m1_script.py, the reference
implementation):
1. Partitioning -- build a qubit interaction graph weighted by
   2-qubit gate count, split it into blocks via Louvain community
   detection (or a Metis/Kernighan-Lin alternative for bounded block
   sizes).
2. Per-block optimization -- evolves a population of gate sequences
   per block over three objectives: maximize fidelity, minimize
   circuit depth, minimize gate-count cost. Two interchangeable
   algorithms are now available: NSGA-II (the original approach) and
   SMS-EMOA (new, see above); both also support swapping in different
   mutation-operator combinations and an optional local fine-tuning
   pass after the search.
3. Reassembly -- optimized blocks are stitched back into a full
   circuit in original gate order, preserving the gates that
   originally crossed block boundaries.
4. Inter-block gate re-injection -- since block-local optimization
   loses some cross-block entanglement, two interchangeable
   strategies (simulated annealing, or greedy fidelity-driven trial-
   and-keep) re-add cross-block gates that recover fidelity.
5. Compression -- gate-cancellation/merging passes plus a standard
   Qiskit transpiler pass.
Quality is tracked throughout via multi-objective indicators
(hypervolume, spread, spacing, epsilon indicator) rather than just a
single best-fitness score.

Fidelity computation (the accuracy metric the whole pipeline
optimizes against) is exact at the current 8-qubit scale: comparing
two circuits' full 2^n x 2^n matrices is cheap and fast at this size,
so it is not an engineering concern for the results being produced
right now.

Extending fidelity computation past exact-matrix range (roughly
10-12+ qubits, needed to eventually push the benchmark suite to
larger circuits) was explored: an approximate Monte-Carlo SWAP-test
estimator (circuit-based, run under a tensor-network/matrix-product-
state simulator) was built and worked well for weakly-connected test
circuits up to 20 qubits, but turned out to become expensive again for
circuits with genuine, non-trivial entanglement (e.g. QAOA-style
circuits), since the tensor-network simulator's cost depends on how
entangled the circuits being compared are, not just on qubit count.
This is now a known, deferred problem rather than an open blocker on
current work -- likely fixes (computing the approximate fidelity only
over the affected qubits/blocks, or preferring exact computation more
often at borderline sizes) are recorded for when scaling is picked
back up.

Engineering infrastructure built to support running many experiments
reliably on laptop hardware:
- A CLI experiment runner (run_experiment.py) that runs the pipeline
  once for a given circuit/algorithm configuration and seed, writing
  its config, metrics, and resulting circuit to an isolated output
  directory.
- A resumable multi-seed sweep runner (run_sweep.py) that runs many
  configurations as isolated subprocesses (so one crashing/hanging
  run cannot take down the rest) and can be stopped and resumed
  without re-running completed configurations.
- A results aggregator (aggregate_results.py) that collects all runs
  into a single table for analysis and figure generation.
- A pytest smoke-test suite that catches pipeline-breaking
  regressions in seconds, before committing to a long run.
- A fixed benchmark suite of five standard circuit families (random
  weakly-connected circuits, QAOA MaxCut, W-state preparation, QFT,
  and a hardware-efficient ansatz), currently run at 8 qubits, used to
  compare algorithms/strategies consistently across experiments.
