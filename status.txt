STATUS REPORT
Quantum Circuit Optimization with Genetic Algorithms (PFE)
Date: 2026-08-24

=====================================================================
OBJECTIVE
=====================================================================
The goal of this project is to make quantum circuit optimization
scale to larger circuits than standard methods currently allow.

Given a target quantum circuit, we split it into smaller blocks of
qubits, then use a genetic algorithm (multi-objective evolutionary
search) to evolve, for each block, a cheaper and shallower circuit
that still reproduces the original circuit's behavior with high
fidelity. The blocks are then reassembled into a full circuit that
is less costly to run while remaining close to the original.

This targets a practical need in near-term quantum computing:
today's devices are noisy and have limited qubit connectivity, so
shallower, cheaper circuits that preserve accuracy are directly
valuable, and are hard to obtain with standard (non-partitioned,
single-objective) optimization approaches at scale.

=====================================================================
CURRENT STATUS
=====================================================================
The core optimization pipeline is implemented and working: circuit
partitioning, per-block evolutionary optimization, reassembly, and
a re-injection step that restores accuracy lost during partitioning.

Over the past weeks, effort has focused on turning this from a
research pipeline into something that can reliably produce results
for a paper on a laptop-only compute budget: reproducible
experiment runs, automated multi-run sweeps across circuit types and
problem sizes, and result aggregation for analysis.

A benchmark suite covering several standard quantum circuit types
(used in quantum chemistry, optimization, and communication
applications) has been built and is being run at increasing circuit
sizes to characterize how well the approach scales. This is
currently in progress; a scaling limitation was identified this week
for certain circuit types and is being addressed before continuing.

=====================================================================
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
=====================================================================
Stack: Qiskit for circuit construction/simulation, DEAP for the
evolutionary algorithm (NSGA-II / NSGA-III multi-objective search).

Pipeline stages (M1_finale/final_m1_script.py, the reference
implementation):
1. Partitioning -- build a qubit interaction graph weighted by
   2-qubit gate count, split it into blocks via Louvain community
   detection (or a Metis/Kernighan-Lin alternative for bounded block
   sizes).
2. Per-block optimization -- NSGA-II evolves a population of gate
   sequences per block over three objectives: maximize fidelity,
   minimize circuit depth, minimize gate-count cost.
3. Reassembly -- optimized blocks are stitched back into a full
   circuit in original gate order, preserving the gates that
   originally crossed block boundaries.
4. Inter-block gate re-injection -- since block-local optimization
   loses some cross-block entanglement, two interchangeable
   strategies (simulated annealing, or greedy fidelity-driven trial-
   and-keep) re-add cross-block gates that recover fidelity.
5. Compression -- gate-cancellation/merging passes plus a standard
   Qiskit transpiler pass.
Quality is tracked throughout via multi-objective indicators
(hypervolume, spread, spacing, epsilon indicator) rather than just a
single best-fitness score.

Fidelity computation (the accuracy metric the whole pipeline
optimizes against) is the main current engineering challenge.
Comparing two circuits exactly requires building their full
2^n x 2^n matrices, which is only tractable up to ~10-12 qubits. Past
that, we use an approximate Monte-Carlo SWAP-test estimator (circuit-
based, run under a tensor-network/matrix-product-state simulator)
instead of the exact computation. This unblocked runs up to 20
qubits for weakly-connected test circuits, but this week we found it
becomes very expensive again for circuits with genuine, non-trivial
entanglement (e.g. QAOA-style circuits), because the tensor-network
simulator's cost depends on how entangled the circuits being compared
are, not just on qubit count. This is the scaling limitation
currently being worked on -- likely fix is to compute the approximate
fidelity only over the affected qubits/blocks instead of the whole
circuit, or to prefer the exact computation more often at the
(still-tractable) sizes currently being benchmarked.

Engineering infrastructure built to support running many experiments
reliably on laptop hardware:
- A CLI experiment runner (run_experiment.py) that runs the pipeline
  once for a given circuit/algorithm configuration and seed, writing
  its config, metrics, and resulting circuit to an isolated output
  directory.
- A resumable multi-seed sweep runner (run_sweep.py) that runs many
  configurations as isolated subprocesses (so one crashing/hanging
  run cannot take down the rest) and can be stopped and resumed
  without re-running completed configurations.
- A results aggregator (aggregate_results.py) that collects all runs
  into a single table for analysis and figure generation.
- A pytest smoke-test suite that catches pipeline-breaking
  regressions in seconds, before committing to a long run.
- A fixed benchmark suite of five standard circuit families (random
  weakly-connected circuits, QAOA MaxCut, W-state preparation, QFT,
  and a hardware-efficient ansatz) at multiple qubit counts, used to
  characterize scaling behavior consistently across experiments.
